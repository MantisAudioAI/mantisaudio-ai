# Developer Log #05 — Beyond the Clone

### AI made building synths easy. Now what do we build?

In [Developer Log #01](https://mantisaudioai.github.io/mantisaudio-ai/Articles/devlog-01), I wrote about chasing the organic sound in a digital world: blending sampled acoustic attacks with synthesis to make instruments that are neither fully real nor fully synthetic.

Since then, I've been thinking about the bigger picture. Because something is happening right now in music software that I don't think we're talking about enough.

**AI has made building a synthesizer easy.**

Oscillators, filters, envelopes, a keyboard, a decent-looking interface. With a good coding assistant, a developer can have a working synth running in an afternoon. That's genuinely amazing. A few years ago, this took serious DSP knowledge and months of work.

But I think we're about to see a flood of "MY CUSTOM ANALOG SYNTH" and "DIGITAL SYNTH CLONE #12."

#### The past is a great teacher

Let me be clear: looking to the past is a great place to start. Who doesn't want the drums, synths, and effects from their favorite records? Studying how classic instruments worked is how most of us learn. Clones keep great designs alive and put sounds in the hands of people who could never afford the originals.

And I'm not pointing fingers. I've built my share of tributes to classic samplers, drum machines, and grooveboxes. I love that gear. I'm guilty too.

But a tribute should be a starting point, not the destination.

#### The endless chase

Here's what worries me. When modern DAWs arrived with multi-gigabyte sampled pianos and orchestral string libraries, something shifted. We gained incredible realism. But somewhere along the way, a lot of the creative spark in instrument design went missing.

It started to feel like an endless chase: recreate every piece of 1970s, '80s, and '90s gear, one model at a time. Perfect emulations of instruments that already exist.

AI can make that chase faster than ever. **The risk is that we use the most powerful creative tools in history to build the same instruments over and over.**

The engineers we're copying weren't trying to copy anyone. They were working with new technology and asking what it could do. Sampling, FM, PCM, wavetables: those were brand-new ideas once. The real tribute to them is to do the same thing with the tools we have now.

#### What's next

I don't have all the answers, but I do believe new synthesis approaches are still waiting to be discovered. A few directions I keep coming back to:

- **Hybrid instruments.** Blending synthesis with natural, human instruments, the idea from Developer Log #01. A bowed attack that blooms into a sound no string could make. A real piano hammer feeding a resonator that doesn't exist in the physical world. I just thinking that adding elements of sounds you find in your own studio or outside are inherently you and bring immense character to your project.
- **Impossible physics.** Physical modeling doesn't have to model real objects. What does a glass drum sound like? A bell the size of a building? A string made of water?
- **Instruments with behavior.** Probability, memory, and context, the ideas from Developer Log #03 on drum machines. Instruments that respond to the music around them instead of repeating the same output for the same input.
- **New interfaces, new gestures.** Every classic instrument shaped how people played it. New ways of touching and controlling sound will lead to music that couldn't be played before.

Not every experiment will work. Most won't. That's the point.

#### A challenge to developers

If you're building music software with AI right now, you're in a remarkable position. The hard technical barrier is lower than it's ever been. That frees up your time for the part that actually matters: **ideas.**

So build the clone if you want to. Learn from it. Then ask what the original designers would have asked: *what can this technology do that nothing before it could?*

**AI helps me build faster than I ever could alone. But it can't decide what's worth building. That part is still on us.**

I'll keep sharing experiments, the ones that work and the ones that don't, as I try to answer that question myself.
